Two ways in, one chronicle out

The hard part was never the writing. It's getting four hours of six people talking over each other into text at all. There are two roads to that, and they both end in the same place — pick the one that matches the hardware you already own.

Record it and transcribe it yourself

Drop in a Discord multitrack, a Zoom export, or a phone recording from the middle of the table. Whisper transcribes it on your own machine — the audio never goes anywhere. Record each player to their own track and every line in the finished chronicle stays attached to the right character, because one track means one person and nothing has to guess.

What you get back

One run over one recording produces all five of these. Open any of them to see what it actually looks like — and none of it is a wall of transcript.

The chronicle the main event · prose, not minutes

Your session written up as a continuous narrative — what the party did, what it cost them, and what they said while doing it. A consistent voice from start to finish, exported as .docx or Markdown and ready to drop into a campaign journal.

The part that matters: it's grounded against your own lore first. Before a word of narrative is written, a separate pass corrects the transcript against a glossary of your names, places and factions — or, if you keep your campaign in Obsidian, against the vault itself. Speech-to-text renders unfamiliar fantasy names phonetically and a general-purpose model will happily invent a plausible spelling and then commit to it for forty pages. This is the step that stops that happening, and everything downstream inherits it.

Grounding from an Obsidian vault

A condensed version you choose how short

The same session at whatever length suits you, written from the finished chronicle rather than from the raw transcript — so shortening it drops the padding rather than the good bits.

A slider sets the target and shows the projected word count as you drag it. A three-hour session lands at roughly 16,000–18,000 words in full; at the default setting the condensed version comes back nearer 3,000. Useful when you want something readable in a coffee break, or a "previously on…" to read aloud at the top of next week's game.

A bullet-point catch-up recap for the Monday Discord post

The events of the session as a short list of plain statements — no prose, no flourish. This is the one you paste into your group chat so the player who missed last week can catch up in thirty seconds, and the one you skim yourself five minutes before the next session starts.

The Gallery of Jests and Gore the bit people actually reread

Two curated lists kept side by side. Jests collects the moments the table actually laughed at. Gore collects the grisly ones — the critical hit that went far too well, the description nobody expected you to commit to.

These get lost faster than anything else. A month later nobody can remember how the joke went, only that there was one. Having them written down is, honestly, what makes people ask for the write-up in the first place.

Memorable quotes sorted funny · stupid · dark

Attributed lines, grouped by flavour, ready to paste. The useful design decision here is that it captures the whole exchange, not single lines — table humour is almost never one sentence. It's the setup, the terrible response, and the pause afterwards. Pulling out just the punchline leaves something that isn't funny to anyone who wasn't there.

Every one of these is optional and every one is editable before you export. If you only ever use the chronicle and the quotes, that's a perfectly sensible way to run it. See everything it does →

What you'll need

Worth reading before you clone anything. Three tiers, so you can see what's genuinely required versus what just makes things better. The summary is below — the full dependencies page covers each one properly, including how to check what you already have.

Critical

Nothing runs without these.

  • Node.js 20+ — the app is a Node program. The one hard prerequisite.
  • Git — how you download it, and how the in-app updater works.
  • One way to reach an AI model — an API key, a Claude Code / Codex subscription you already pay for, or a local model. Pick one; they're interchangeable.

You do not need Visual Studio Build Tools. Nothing here compiles.

Advised

Not required — but the result is noticeably better.

  • Python 3.10–3.12 — runs Whisper to transcribe your recordings locally. 3.13 is untested rather than broken.
  • An NVIDIA GPU specifically — turns "several hours" of transcription into 20–30 minutes. AMD, Intel and Apple GPUs aren't used at all; the engine has no backend for them.
  • Discord + Craig — records one track per person, which is what makes speaker attribution reliable. Craig's free tier is enough.

Nice to have

Quality of life. Skip freely.

  • Obsidian — a pleasant home for campaign lore, though any folder of markdown works.
  • Claude Code / Codex CLI — spends a subscription you already have instead of API credit.
  • Ollama or LM Studio — run some or all phases offline.
  • Windows Terminal — a tidier launcher window. Purely cosmetic.

Before you install — what to expect

The honest version: how long a session actually takes, what hardware it wants, and where the output is weaker. Better to know now than after cloning.

Measured on a real session — 8 players, 3 hours of audio, NVIDIA RTX 3070 Ti
Stage How long Needs you there?
Local Whisper transcription 20–30 minutes No
Six-phase chronicle pipeline ~30 minutes No — pause and resume are built in
Total, end to end About an hour Start it and walk away

Whisper transcription is heavily GPU-dependent

Those numbers assume an NVIDIA GPU, and that word is doing real work. Whisper here runs on faster-whisper, whose backend implements exactly two devices: CUDA and CPU. There is no AMD, Intel Arc or Apple GPU backend — those cards aren't used at all, so you get the same speed as a machine with no graphics card. That's the engine's limitation, not a preference.

On the processor, a three-hour session can take several hours rather than half an hour. It still works if you set it going and walk away. Otherwise use the YouTube route below — it does the transcribing on Google's hardware instead of yours.

Craig + Whisper best output

Record in Discord with Craig, drop the multi-track zip in, and Whisper transcribes each speaker's track separately on your machine. Because every track is one person, attribution is reliable from the transcript all the way through to the finished chronicle.

Needs: an NVIDIA GPU (realistically) plus the Audio Transcription add-on. AMD and Intel cards aren't used at all — the engine has no backend for them.

Record and let YouTube transcribe easiest start

Record the session with OBS Studio or any similar recorder, upload it to YouTube as unlisted or private, and let YouTube's automatic captions do the transcription on Google's hardware. No GPU, no Python, no add-on to install.

  1. Record the session (OBS Studio, or whatever you already use).
  2. Upload to YouTube; set visibility to Unlisted or Private.
  3. Wait for automatic captions to finish generating.
  4. In YouTube Studio → your video → Subtitles, take your pick:
    • open the language's caption track and copy the transcript text, then paste it straight into the Raw transcript box in Tomes; or
    • ⋮ → Download .sbv, then drop the file into the Caption Repair tab → Repair captions → Send to refinement.

The .sbv route keeps the timestamps, which helps the pipeline segment the session; pasting the plain text is quicker. Either way you land in the same chronicle pipeline.

The trade-off, stated plainly

The YouTube route is much more beginner-friendly, but the output is not as good, and the gap is entirely about who said what. A YouTube caption file contains no speaker labels at all — it is one undifferentiated stream of text. Tomes reconstructs speakers from context, and to do that it will ask you a fair number of clarifying questions before it can attribute lines confidently. Even then, attribution is a best guess in places, where the multi-track Craig route simply knows.

If you want the chronicle to reliably say which character did what, record multi-track. If you mainly want a readable account of the session and can live with some fuzziness on attribution, YouTube is a perfectly good place to start — and you can always switch later.

How long the output actually is

A three-hour session typically produces a chronicle of 16,000–18,000 words. If that's more than you want to read out to the table, the optional condense phase cuts it down as far as you like — you set the target.

What hasn't been tested much yet

Being straight about maturity, since this is open source and you're the one installing it:

  • Windows is the tested platform. The current build has not been tested on Linux or macOS — it's expected to work, but assume you may hit rough edges, and please report them.
  • The Codex add-on is only partially tested. The provider, routing and usage-limit handling are covered, but it has had far less real-session mileage than the cloud path.
  • Local LLMs have not been tested above 4B parameters, for want of VRAM on the test machine. Everything larger is unverified here.

The setup that works best

Everything is optional and swappable, but if you want the short answer to "what should I actually pick" — this is the combination that has produced the best results in practice.

  1. Record with Craig free tier is enough

    The Craig Discord bot records each participant to their own track. Nothing here needs a paid Craig plan — the free tier covers this workflow.

  2. Transcribe with Whisper, locally

    Strongly recommended alongside Craig, because per-speaker tracks give Whisper audio that is trivially attributable — every line ends up tied to the right person. This is the single biggest quality difference versus any single-stream transcript.

  3. Keep lore in an Obsidian vault

    The Obsidian Vault add-on is recommended over the plain Tusks-Lore folder layout — it grounds names and lore more reliably. In head-to-head testing the outputs were comparable, so the folder route is far from useless; Obsidian is just the better of the two.

  4. Route Gemini + Claude Code

    The best balance found so far between cost and output quality. Part of the reason is unglamorous but important: Gemini's API lets you turn the content filters off, so the mature material a real session contains — violence, swearing, explicit dialogue — is processed and written up as it happened. Claude and ChatGPT models tend to sanitise that content by default, and no amount of prompt wording fully prevents it.

How it works

A six-phase pipeline. Each phase does one job and hands off to the next, so a failure is recoverable and you can pause a run and pick it up later.

  1. Get the words out of the recording

    Drop in a Craig multi-track Discord zip, a single audio file from Zoom or a phone, a YouTube .sbv caption file, or just paste text. Audio is transcribed locally by Whisper — one track per speaker, so attribution survives.

    {{CLIP:import}}

  2. Ground the names the important bit

    Speech-to-text renders unfamiliar fantasy names phonetically, and a general LLM will invent a spelling and commit to it. Phase 1 corrects the raw transcript against your own glossary of characters, places and terms first, so everything downstream is working from your canon.

    {{CLIP:speakers}}

  3. Flag what's ambiguous

    The audit phase compares the raw and corrected transcripts and hands the DM a list of things it could not resolve, rather than guessing and burying the guess in prose.

  4. Write the chronicle

    The narrative pass turns the grounded transcript into continuous prose, keeping player dialogue where it matters and rendering the DM's narration as narration.

    {{CLIP:run}}

  5. Pull the extras

    Quotes, jests and combat highlights are extracted as structured lists in a separate pass.

  6. Condense

    Optionally boil the chronicle down to the short recap version for posting.

Built for a different job

Otter.ai, Read.ai and Descript are genuinely good tools, and if you want a searchable record of a standup they will beat this comfortably. They are built around the shape of a meeting — decisions, owners, action items. A session has none of those and is mostly made of invented proper nouns, so the two want quite different things.

Who it's for

  • Game masters running long campaigns who want every session written up without doing it themselves at 2am.
  • Groups on Discord recording with Craig and wanting per-player attribution that survives to the finished text.
  • Actual-play podcasters and streamers who need episode recaps, show notes and pull-quotes without paying an editor to listen back.
  • VTT archivists on Roll20, Foundry or Fantasy Grounds — paste the chat log, no audio required.
  • Anyone who won't upload campaign audio to a SaaS and would rather keep the whole thing on their own disk.

What it costs

Nothing to install and nothing to subscribe to. The only money involved is whatever your chosen model provider charges for the tokens — and there are three separate ways to reduce that to nothing.

Cloud models

Around £1–£2 per three-hour session on a paid Gemini key. Built-in routing presets trade cost against quality, with the savings shown before you run.

A subscription you already pay for no API spend

If Claude Code or the OpenAI Codex CLI is installed and signed in, Tomes can route phases through it and draw on that plan's existing allowance instead of spending API credit. It uses your logged-in session — API keys are stripped from the call, so nothing lands on a metered bill. If the allowance runs out mid-run the pipeline pauses rather than failing, and resumes when it resets.

Fully local

£0. Route every phase to Ollama, LM Studio or Unsloth and nothing leaves the machine — no key, no bill, no network. Read the prose-quality note below before choosing this for the chronicle phase.

Mixed

Run the mechanical phases on a local model or a subscription CLI, and send only the narrative pass to a cloud one. Most of the quality, a fraction of the spend.

What you give up going fully local

Local models are not as good at long-form narrative prose as the cloud ones, and it shows most in the phase you care about most. Below roughly 15–20B parameters the chronicle tends to read like a log rather than a story — events in sequence, little narrative voice — and grounding against your glossary is less reliable too. Mileage varies a lot between models of similar size.

The pragmatic middle ground is the Mixed option above: run the mechanical phases locally and send Phase 3, the chronicle itself, to a cloud model or a subscription CLI. You keep most of the saving and all of the prose quality.

Does a big lore library make it expensive?

Not really. More lore means more context for the model to read, so cost does rise — but gently. In practice, up to around 100,000 words of lore spread across several documents was not a noticeable difference on the bill. Add the lore; the grounding it buys is worth far more than the tokens it costs.

Why I built this

I wanted a record of my campaign. Not minutes — a record. Something a player who missed a night could read and arrive already caught up, and something you could go back to two years later and actually feel the session again.

The first few I wrote by hand. They were the best ones, they took about four hours each, and I stopped. So I did the obvious thing and handed a transcript to an AI, which worked beautifully right up until it decided the party's patron was called Duskwarden in the first chapter and Dawnwarden by the third, and then stayed confidently wrong for the rest of the write-up. Fixing that by hand took longer than writing it myself would have.

That one stubborn failure is where the whole project comes from. Everything else here — the six phases, the pause-and-resume, the quote lists, the slider that sets how long the recap should be — grew around a single idea: correct the names against the campaign's own lore first, before a sentence of prose exists, and let everything downstream inherit it.

It's a personal project and it reads like one. It does one thing, it does it on hardware you already own, and it will never grow an account, a subscription or a pricing page. If it turns out to be useful at your table as well as mine, that's the best outcome I could ask for — and if it does something annoying, the issue tracker is right there and I do read it.

— KochiTusker, who mostly just wanted the write-ups
