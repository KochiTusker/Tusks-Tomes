I

Why this exists

Everyone says they'll write it up. Almost nobody does.

I wanted a record of my campaign. Not minutes — the actual story. The bit where the plan fell apart in an interesting way, the joke that had everyone crying, the line the DM delivered that made the table go quiet for a second.

The obvious tools didn't fit. They're built for meetings, and they have no idea who your characters are — they hear an invented fantasy name, guess a spelling, and then use that guess with total confidence for four hours straight.

And a session isn't minutes. What you want back is the story: something you would actually read again, the lines worth quoting, and a recap short enough that whoever missed the last one can catch up before they sit down. That is as much use to a player as it is to whoever runs the game.

So I wrote this instead.

III

How it compares

Why not use something that already exists?

Often you should. Most people find this page having already tried a meeting transcriber — good software, built for standups, where nobody is called Vellichor the Pale, nobody needs the joke from hour two written down, and “the group discussed next steps” is a useful sentence.

There is also a set of tools built specifically for tabletop — Tabletop Scribe, DnD Scrybe, Kazkar, SessionKeeper, Saga20 and more arriving. They are made by people who play, several handle speaker attribution well, and they will take a recording without asking you to install anything. If one of them suits your table, use it.

These are the things worth checking first, including the one where this project comes off worse.

Three rows carry the whole argument: where the recording goes, whether attribution is known or guessed, and who decides how it gets written. Everywhere else the tabletop services are genuinely competitive, and the browser row is one they simply win. That is the trade, and it is worth being plain about why it exists — no software can attribute dialogue to the right player unless it is handed something that already knows who was speaking. The only way to know rather than guess is to record each person separately, and that means Discord, a bot and an afternoon of setup you would not otherwise spend.

The last column is the benchmark, not a rival. Someone who actually sits down and writes their session up produces the best version of it there is — the catch is the evening it takes, and that almost nobody does it twice. The middle columns get a fairer hearing than a grid can give them in the full comparison, including the case for using one of them instead.

None of this is about price. It is about the recording staying yours, the names staying right, and you deciding how the story comes out. That is the whole pitch.

IV

How it works

Six passes, each doing one job

A transcript goes in one end. Each pass hands off to the next, so a failure is recoverable and a long run can be paused and picked up tomorrow.

  1. 1

    Ground the names

    Your glossary and lore are applied to the raw transcript first, so everything downstream is working from your canon instead of the model's guesses.

    The reason this exists

  2. 2

    Audit

    Compares raw against grounded and hands you a list of what it couldn't resolve, rather than guessing quietly.

  3. 3

    Write the chronicle

    The narrative pass. Player dialogue stays dialogue; the DM's narration reads as narration.

  4. 4

    Pull the extras

    Quotes, jests and the gorier highlights, extracted as separate lists.

  5. 5

    Polish

    A final review pass, used when you're running the pipeline on a model on your own machine.

  6. 6

    Condense

    Optional. Boils the chronicle down to the short version for posting to the group.

You can send different passes to different models — the cheap ones do the mechanical work, the good one writes the prose. How per-phase routing works →

VI

Getting a session in

Two roads, depending on your hardware

The hard part was never the writing — it's getting four hours of six people talking over each other into text at all. Both roads end at the same grounding pass.

Session audioWhisper, on your PCChronicle

Record it and transcribe it yourself

A Discord multitrack, a Zoom export, or a phone on the table. Whisper runs locally, so the audio never goes anywhere. Record each player to their own track and every line in the finished chronicle stays attached to the right character — one track means one person, and nothing has to guess. It also transcribes what was actually said, swearing included.

Wants an NVIDIA GPU, and one module installed.

The audio route

Session videoYouTube captionsChronicle

Let YouTube do the transcribing

Upload the recording unlisted, wait for the automatic captions, bring the transcript back. The transcription happens on Google's hardware instead of yours. Captions carry no speaker labels and censor swearing, so the audit pass asks more questions and the language comes back tamer than your table — but it needs nothing you do not already have.

Wants nothing. No GPU, no Python, nothing to install.

VIII

What leaves your machine

You're handing this a recording of your friends

So it's worth being exact about where everything goes. Almost all of it stays on the machine you installed it on.

  • No account, no sign-up, no licence check.
  • No telemetry, no analytics, nothing phoning home. Ever.
  • Keys encrypted at rest and tied to the machine — copying the file elsewhere gains nothing.
  • MIT licensed, every line readable, including the setup scripts.

And the honest part: this is one person's project, built and tested on one Windows PC. It is not a company and there is no support desk. The unvarnished version is here.
