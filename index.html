Your campaign, written up properly

Point it at a recording of your D&D, Pathfinder or Call of Cthulhu game — or at a transcript you already have — and get back a narrative account of what actually happened. Spelled the way your table spells things, because it reads your campaign's own lore before it writes a word.

I

Why this exists

Everyone says they'll write it up. Almost nobody does.

I wanted a record of my campaign. Not minutes — the actual story. The bit where the plan fell apart in an interesting way, the joke that had everyone crying, the line the DM delivered that made the table go quiet for a second.

The obvious tools didn't fit. They're built for meetings, and they have no idea who your characters are — they hear an invented fantasy name, guess a spelling, and then use that guess with total confidence for four hours straight.

And a session isn't minutes. What you want back is the story: something you would actually read again, the lines worth quoting, and a recap short enough that whoever missed the last one can catch up before they sit down. That is as much use to a player as it is to whoever runs the game.

So I wrote this instead.

III

How it compares

Why not use something that already exists?

Often you should. Most people find this page having already tried a meeting transcriber — good software, built for standups, where nobody is called Vellichor the Pale, nobody needs the joke from hour two written down, and “the group discussed next steps” is a useful sentence.

There is also a set of tools built specifically for tabletop — Tabletop Scribe, DnD Scrybe, Kazkar, SessionKeeper, Saga20 and more arriving. They are made by people who play, several handle speaker attribution well, and they will take a recording without asking you to install anything. If one of them suits your table, use it.

These are the things worth checking first, including the one where this project comes off worse.

A monthly fee, whether or not you played that week. A free tier, then a monthly fee. An evening. And you would probably do it better.

Three rows carry the whole argument: where the recording goes, whether attribution is known or guessed, and who decides how it gets written. Everywhere else the tabletop services are genuinely competitive, and the browser row is one they simply win. That is the trade, and it is worth being plain about why it exists — no software can attribute dialogue to the right player unless it is handed something that already knows who was speaking. The only way to know rather than guess is to record each person separately, and that means Discord, a bot and an afternoon of setup you would not otherwise spend.

The last column is the benchmark, not a rival. Someone who actually sits down and writes their session up produces the best version of it there is — the catch is the evening it takes, and that almost nobody does it twice. The middle columns get a fairer hearing than a grid can give them in the full comparison, including the case for using one of them instead.

None of this is about price. It is about the recording staying yours, the names staying right, and you deciding how the story comes out. That is the whole pitch.

IV

How it works

Six passes, each doing one job

A transcript goes in one end. Each pass hands off to the next, so a failure is recoverable and a long run can be paused and picked up tomorrow.

  1. 1

    Ground the names

    Your glossary and lore are applied to the raw transcript first, so everything downstream is working from your canon instead of the model's guesses.

    The reason this exists

  2. 2

    Audit

    Compares raw against grounded and hands you a list of what it couldn't resolve, rather than guessing quietly.

  3. 3

    Write the chronicle

    The narrative pass. Player dialogue stays dialogue; the DM's narration reads as narration.

  4. 4

    Pull the extras

    Quotes, jests and the gorier highlights, extracted as separate lists.

  5. 5

    Polish

    A final review pass, used when you're running the pipeline on a model on your own machine.

  6. 6

    Condense

    Optional. Boils the chronicle down to the short version for posting to the group.

You can send different passes to different models — the cheap ones do the mechanical work, the good one writes the prose. How per-phase routing works →

VI

Getting a session in

Two roads, depending on your hardware

The hard part was never the writing — it's getting four hours of six people talking over each other into text at all. Both roads end at the same grounding pass.

Session audioWhisper, on your PCChronicle

Record it and transcribe it yourself

A Discord multitrack, a Zoom export, or a phone on the table. Whisper runs locally, so the audio never goes anywhere. Record each player to their own track and every line in the finished chronicle stays attached to the right character — one track means one person, and nothing has to guess. It also transcribes what was actually said, swearing included.

Wants an NVIDIA GPU, and one module installed.

Let YouTube do the transcribing

Upload the recording unlisted, wait for the automatic captions, bring the transcript back. The transcription happens on Google's hardware instead of yours. Captions carry no speaker labels and censor swearing, so the audit pass asks more questions and the language comes back tamer than your table — but it needs nothing you do not already have.

VIII

What leaves your machine

You're handing this a recording of your friends

So it's worth being exact about where everything goes. Almost all of it stays on the machine you installed it on.

  • No account, no sign-up, no licence check.
  • No telemetry, no analytics, nothing phoning home. Ever.
  • Keys encrypted at rest and tied to the machine — copying the file elsewhere gains nothing.
  • MIT licensed, every line readable, including the setup scripts.

And the honest part: this is one person's project, built and tested on one Windows PC. It is not a company and there is no support desk. The unvarnished version is here.

IX

Questions

The ones people actually ask

Why would I choose Tusk’s Tomes?
Because of where it runs, and what it is fed. There are good tools built for tabletop now — several ground names against your lore much as this does, and if one of them suits your table you should use it. What none of them do is leave the recording on your own machine. Tusk’s Tomes transcribes locally and corrects the transcript against your own glossary before any prose is written, so the write-up spells your world the way your table spells it. It takes per-speaker Craig tracks, so who said what is known rather than inferred from one mixed recording. And you choose the model, the narrator and the prompts — it is MIT-licensed, so if the answer you want is not in the settings, it is in the source.
I’m new to this. Where do I start?
Install Node and Git, clone the repository, and run the setup script — that is the whole of it, and the installation guide walks through each step. If you have never used a terminal, there is a version of the same setup written for you that assumes nothing. Start by pasting in a transcript you already have; audio can wait until you have seen what it produces.
How much better is the Craig and Whisper route?
Two ways, and the second one surprises people. Attribution: Craig records each player to a separate track, so Whisper transcribes one voice at a time and every line stays attached to the right character with nothing having to guess. A YouTube caption file is one undifferentiated stream with no speaker labels at all, so the pipeline reconstructs who spoke from context and asks you a good many more questions before it can. Language: YouTube’s automatic captions censor swearing — profanity comes back as [ __ ], already gone by the time you download the file, and nothing downstream can put it back. Whisper transcribes what was actually said. If your table swears, and the way people really talk is part of what you want on the record, the multitrack route is the only one that keeps it.
I don’t have an NVIDIA GPU. Can I still transcribe audio?
Today, in two ways: let YouTube transcribe the recording on Google’s hardware and bring the text back, or compile a whisper.cpp build, which can use an AMD, Intel or Apple GPU. Cloud transcription is also in progress and will remove the requirement entirely — it is measured at roughly $0.20 for a three-hour, five-speaker session, and will need only an OpenRouter key. See the roadmap for where that has got to.
What is OpenRouter, and why does Tusk’s Tomes use it?
OpenRouter is a single API that fronts most of the commercial model landscape — around 400 models, including every Anthropic and OpenAI model, at the same prices the vendors charge directly. One key reaches all of them. Tusk’s Tomes used to carry separate integrations for each vendor, each with its own key to manage and its own model list to keep current; one OpenRouter key replaced all of that and opened several hundred models that were never reachable before. Gemini stays available as a direct connection because it is measurably cheaper that way.
How do I choose which model to use through OpenRouter?
The picker does the narrowing for you. It offers three orderings — by measured performance, by what that particular phase would cost, and by the maintainer’s own ranking, which weighs speed and reliability that no grade captures — and they deliberately disagree. Two filters cut the catalogue down: tested only restricts it to models actually graded on real session material, and handles mature content to models measured relaying graphic violence and crude dialogue without softening them. If you would rather not choose at all, the guided routing presets are complete recipes you can apply in one click.
Is it actually better than a paid app, or just cheaper?
Cheaper is not the argument, and against the tabletop-native services it is not reliably true either — several sell credit by the session much as this spends your own API credit. Against a meeting transcriber it is doing a different job: those produce a transcript and bullet points about what was discussed, with no mechanism for knowing your characters’ names. Against the tools built for tabletop, the differences are narrower and worth stating plainly — your audio never leaves your machine, per-speaker Craig tracks mean attribution is known rather than inferred, and you pick which model writes each phase. On chronicle quality specifically, the honest answer is that it depends on which model you route to: the strongest routings tested — a subscription CLI on the mechanical passes with Gemini Pro writing the prose — produce the best output measured so far, while the OpenRouter catalogue ranges from noticeably worse to better than Gemini on individual phases. The project deliberately does not publish a single quality score, because chronicle quality is not one number and any tool that claims otherwise is flattering itself.
What actually gets put on my PC?
The repository itself, plus node_modules inside it. Outside that folder it writes one configuration directory in the standard place for your operating system, holding your glossary, routing choices and encrypted keys, and a data directory for sessions if you transcribe audio. That is all, unless you install the transcription module — which adds a Python environment of about 1.5 GB, inside the repository folder. No PATH changes, no registry entries, no system-wide packages, and nothing installed as administrator. The full list, file by file.
How can I keep track of what it changes on my machine?
Everything it writes outside its own folder is documented in one place, and there is an uninstall script that removes it. The design rule is that installing something must never change anything outside the repository working directory, and uninstalling must remove every byte the install wrote — your system Python, Node, PATH and registry are left alone. That is written down as a contract rather than a promise: see module isolation for what a module may and may not touch, and what it installs for the complete inventory.
How can I support the project?
Use it and tell me what broke — that is genuinely the most useful thing, and the feedback form is the fastest route. Starring the repository helps other people find it, bug reports and pull requests are welcome, and if you would like to put money towards it there is Buy Me a Coffee. None of it unlocks anything — there is no paid tier and there will not be one.
